Nº 003 — The mirror

White sends the sun back.

In short Ice is a mirror that keeps the Arctic cool. Open ocean is a dark sponge that soaks up heat.

Snow-covered ice reflects about 85% of the sunlight that hits it. Dark open ocean absorbs more than 90%. Slide from ice to ocean and watch where the sunlight goes.

Nº 004 — The dangers

Melting makes more melting.

In short Less ice means more heat, and more heat means less ice. It feeds itself.

  1. The air gets warmer. Greenhouse gases trap more heat around the planet.
  2. The ice melts. Summers last longer and more ice disappears.
  3. Dark water appears. Where the white mirror was, there is now open ocean.
  4. The ocean soaks up heat. It warms the air and the water, so even more ice melts next year.

What it puts at risk

  • A hotter planetThe Arctic is warming about four times faster than the rest of the world, and that extra heat adds to global warming.
  • Coasts falling into the seaSea ice protects Arctic shores from waves. Without it, storms eat away the coast. Some villages in Alaska are having to move.
  • Thawing groundA warmer Arctic thaws the frozen ground on land, the permafrost. It can release more greenhouse gases.
  • Weather further southScientists are studying whether a warmer Arctic makes some storms, heatwaves and cold snaps further south more likely.
  • Arctic wildlifePolar bears, seals and walruses need the ice to hunt, rest and raise their young. See below.

Nº 005 — The glass

It does not flood. It does something else.

In short Melting sea ice does not raise the sea. Melting ice from land does.

Sea ice is frozen sea water that already floats. When it melts, the sea level stays the same, like an ice cube in a glass. Ice from land, like Greenland’s ice sheet, is different: when it slides into the ocean, the water rises. Try both.

Nº 006 — Old ice

Younger, thinner, weaker.

In short The ice is now younger and thinner, so it breaks and melts more easily.

Ice that survives several summers grows thick and strong, up to 4 metres. Young ice, made in one winter, is often only 1 to 2 metres thick. The Arctic has lost most of its old ice.

Nº 007 — Who lives on the ice

A floor, a roof, a pantry.

In short No ice, no hunting ground, no safe place for the young.

For many animals, sea ice is not scenery. It is where they hunt, rest and raise their young. Click a card to see what changes when the ice goes.

    Nº 008 — The first blue summer

    An Arctic without ice.

    In short Soon, some summers will have almost no ice left around the North Pole.

    Scientists call the Arctic “ice-free” when less than 1 million km² is left, mostly along the coasts of Greenland and Canada. The IPCC says this will likely happen in September at least once before 2050, whatever we do now. What we do decides how often it happens after that.
